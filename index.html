Vol. 01A field guideUpdated Oct 2026

AI agents are getting to work.

Between 2024 and 2026, AI agents went from demos to doing real jobs — writing code, answering customers, using computers, trading and paying for things on their own. This guide covers what they are, what they run on, where they work, how the money moves, and what could go wrong.

Compiled by SamG1k
Every claim sourced — see §9
~20 min read
§0

Start here

The basics, in plain English
0.1

What is the agentic economy?

Today, people do the searching, comparing, booking and paying. In an agentic economy, AI agents do more of that work. Microsoft researchers describe it as an economy “where assistant and service agents interact programmatically” to get transactions done.[48]

An assistant agent works for you: it carries your preferences to businesses. A service agent works for a business, dealing with customers and other companies.[48] The agentic economy is what happens when those agents start finding each other, negotiating and paying.

Microsoft’s researchers expect it to change how things are sold. When agents handle payments, the hassle of tiny payments disappears — favouring pay-per-use over subscriptions — and competition shifts from grabbing human attention to matching agents with the best services.[48]

Our current trajectory points toward the accidental emergence of a vast, and likely permeable, sandbox economy.Google DeepMind researchers, on agents trading with each other in markets that leak into ours — Sep 2025[50]

0.4

How much does it do on its own?

Five levels of autonomy
L1 · Operator
  1. L1
    Operator

    You lead. The agent helps only when called or with suggestions you approve.

  2. L2
    Collaborator

    You and the agent plan and do the work together, often in parallel.

  3. L3
    Consultant

    The agent takes the lead; you give feedback and direction.

  4. L4
    Approver

    The agent works on its own and checks in only for blockers or big decisions.

  5. L5
    Observer

    The agent runs fully on its own. You watch the logs and keep an off-switch.

Levels by Feng, McDonald and Zhang, defined by the role the person plays. A very capable agent can still be built to sit at a low level.[52]

0.5

Why agents need their own money

“An AI agent isn’t a legal person,” so it can’t hold a bank account in its own name, and its authority changes every time it gets new instructions or a new budget.[53] Agents also need systems that don’t make them get around CAPTCHAs, two-factor prompts and approvals built for humans.[24] Hence wallets, stablecoins and payment tokens scoped to one job.[8][13]

0.6

Common questions

Is this just crypto?
No. Crypto is one payment rail — x402 and Tempo settle in stablecoins.[8][20] But Visa, Mastercard, Stripe, PayPal and ChatGPT’s checkout run agent payments over ordinary cards and accounts.[7][13][30]
Will agents take jobs?
Some work, already — Klarna’s support assistant is one example (§3).[35] Gartner expects 15% of day-to-day work decisions to be made by agents by 2028.[43] Google DeepMind researchers list job substitution and polarisation among the main risks.[50]
Is it safe to let an agent spend my money?
There are guardrails — spending limits, approvals, payment tokens locked to one shop and one amount — but the risk often sits with you, and prompt injection has no complete fix. More in §5.[17][19][46]
Who’s in charge — me or the agent?
It depends how the agent is built (see 0.4).[52] Most products today keep a person in the loop: Operator pauses before actions with outside effects, and GitHub’s agent hands back a pull request to review.[37][38]
Will it be open, or owned by a few giants?
Undecided. Microsoft researchers describe two futures: “agentic walled gardens” run by dominant platforms, or an open “web of agents” where agents can freely connect and transact. They argue for the open one.[48]

§1

The agents

What they can do

In 2024, agents went from demo to product. Cognition pitched Devin as an AI software engineer that could plan and carry out complex coding tasks.[34] Anthropic let Claude use a computer the way people do — looking at the screen, moving a cursor, clicking and typing — while admitting the ability was “imperfect”.[36]

OpenAI’s Operator could book a restaurant table or fill a grocery order in a browser, but scored 38.1% on a benchmark of computer tasks where humans score 72.4%.[37] The gap is closing fast: METR found the length of tasks agents can complete has doubled roughly every seven months for six years.[45]

The next generation of agents won’t just advise — they’ll act.Erik Reppel & Josh Nickerson, Coinbase — Cointelegraph, Feb 2026

Not everything sold as an agent is one. Gartner estimates only about 130 of the thousands of “agentic AI” vendors are the real thing, and expects over 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by the end of 2027 — over cost, unclear value and weak risk controls.[43]

Brian Armstrong’s argument in 2024: agents are held back because they can’t pay, and crypto wallets give them a way to transact.[2] The rest of this guide follows the thread — the infrastructure agents run on, the work they do, how the money moves, and who is accountable when it goes wrong.

The infrastructure

The stack, top layer to bottom
L1
Models & agents

The AI that plans and acts — Claude, OpenAI’s computer-using agent, Devin.[36][37][34]

L2

MCP connects agents to tools; A2A lets agents work together.[44][6]

L3
Platforms

Where agents are built and run — AWS AgentCore, Salesforce Agentforce, GitHub.[42][39][38]

L4

Identity and verification — ERC-8004, Visa’s TAP, Mastercard’s agent checks.[10][28][7]

L5
Money

Payment protocols and wallets — x402, AP2, ACP, MPP, stablecoins.[8][11][13][20]

MCP became the common plug. In December 2025 Anthropic donated it to the new Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation, co-founded with OpenAI and Block. By then it had more than 10,000 public servers and 97M+ monthly SDK downloads, and was used by ChatGPT, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Cursor and VS Code.[44]

The cloud giants are packaging the rest of the plumbing. AWS’s AgentCore bundles what agents need to run in production — runtime, memory, identity, a browser tool, a code interpreter and monitoring — and launched in preview in July 2025.[42]

How an agent pays

x402, step by step
AGENTSERVER
01
Request

The agent asks for something — an API, a dataset, a page.

02
402 Payment Required

Instead of the content, the server replies with payment instructions.

03
Payment authorization

The agent pays — in stablecoins, settled near-instantly.

04
Access + confirmation

Once the payment is verified, the agent gets what it asked for.[12]

That’s the whole loop. No account, no subscription, no API key. x402 reuses HTTP’s “402 Payment Required” code — long unused — so payment becomes part of an ordinary web request.[8][12]

The examples given at the x402 Foundation launch: an AI assistant buying from several merchants at once, agents paying per browser session, trading bots buying real-time data through micropayments.[12] By February 2026 x402 had reportedly processed 50 million transactions.[15]

4.3

The money behind it

Big money is betting on it. McKinsey forecasts agentic commerce could reach $3–5 trillion globally by 2030 Forecast[25], and Gartner expects a third of enterprise software apps to include agentic AI by 2028 Forecast.[43] Visa says AI-driven traffic to US retail sites rose more than 4,700% in a year.[28]

The payment companies are buying in. Stripe closed its $1.1B purchase of stablecoin firm Bridge in February 2025, its largest acquisition, and then bought wallet maker Privy.[47][9] Mastercard agreed to pay up to $1.8B for stablecoin firm BVNK.[29]

§5

Trust & governance

What isn’t solved yet

  1. Who approved it?

    If an agent buys the wrong thing, who pays? Google built AP2 around three questions: did the user approve this purchase, can the merchant trust the request reflects what the user wanted, and who is responsible for fraud or mistakes.[11]

  2. How autonomous is “autonomous”?

    Truth Terminal, the bot that got $50K from Marc Andreessen, had limited autonomy: its human handler approved its posts and decided who it talked to.[1]

  3. Can agents be tricked?

    AI jailbreaker Pliny the Prompter tried to get Truth Terminal to send him the funds. Its creator rejected the attempt and the bot refused.[1]

  4. Can agents be hijacked?

    Prompt injection — hiding instructions in content an agent reads — tops OWASP’s list of AI security risks for the third year running. “Excessive agency”, agents holding more permissions than they need, jumped to third. OWASP’s co-chair says prompt injection is “something organizations must continuously manage rather than expect to eliminate.”[46]

  5. Who holds the keys?

    The answers so far are limits set in advance. ChatGPT’s checkout uses payment tokens scoped to one merchant and one cart total, so the agent never touches stored card details.[13] Coinbase’s Agentic Wallets let users set permissions before the agent acts.[15]

  6. Who checks the agents?

    ERC-8004 proposes independent validators who stake to check an agent’s claims — rewarded if right, slashed if wrong. It is still under review.[10] Mastercard requires agents to be verified before they can pay.[7] Visa’s Trusted Agent Protocol, built with Cloudflare, lets merchants tell approved agents from malicious bots: “We believe the entire payments ecosystem has a responsibility to ensure sellers can trust AI agents.”[28]

  7. Whose rails win?

    x402, AP2, the Agentic Commerce Protocol, Visa and Mastercard all launched in 2025. Several are designed to stay open: AP2 is payment-agnostic, ACP is meant to work with multiple processors, and x402 plans to support cards and bank accounts.[11][13][12]

  8. Who’s liable when it goes wrong?

    For now, mostly you. Robinhood’s terms: “You assume all risk for trades executed by AI agents and for any use of your data by third-party LLM providers.” Fed Governor Christopher Waller has named authentication, liability and fraud as barriers to agents handling consumer payments.[19]

  9. Who regulates it?

    FINRA’s 2026 oversight report covers AI agents for the first time but adds no new rules, calling existing ones “technologically neutral”.[19] The Financial Stability Board has proposed non-binding practices, warning that undoing an agent’s actions “can be difficult or impossible for humans.”[33]
